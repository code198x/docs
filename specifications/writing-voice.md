# Writing voice

Write as a knowledgeable, friendly collaborator exploring a subject with the reader: precise, curious and encouraging, without fuss or condescension. Apply this voice across Foundations, Maths for Games, The Craft, assets and system tracks.

## Audience

Write for everyone, regardless of age, background, identity or experience. Beginners and experienced readers are equally welcome, as are people arriving through play, art, music, design, history or programming. Assume intelligence, not prior knowledge or an ambition to become a programmer.

In early material, use an Usborne-style approach: concrete examples, short purposeful explanations and illustrations that do explanatory work. Technical depth may increase; clarity and respect remain constant. Explain prerequisites and actual equipment needs. Use readable structure, useful text equivalents and alternatives where feasible; do not claim an activity is accessible without checking it. See the charter's [audience commitment](../PROJECT.md#audience-and-voice).

## Language

- Use British English; use “program” for computer software.
- Prefer “we” when working through ideas together. Use “you” for instructions and choices.
- Explain unfamiliar terms when they first matter. Use proper vocabulary with enough context to understand it.
- Explain how and why. Concision must not create an unexplained jump.
- Use clear headings and focused explanations so experienced readers can find what they need.
- Remove unnecessary introductions, repeated summaries, filler and decorative jargon. Every paragraph should explain, demonstrate, connect or help the reader act.
- Avoid “obviously”, “simply”, “as everyone knows” and language that treats uncertainty as a failing.
- Allow light, inclusive humour. Avoid forced jokes, sarcasm aimed at the reader, profanity and references that require belonging to a particular generation or culture.

## Examples and connections

Choose examples suitable for contemporary education. Review historical examples rather than copying stereotypes or offensive conventions. A reader should not need cultural familiarity to understand the programming.

Games from different eras and physical forms may supply examples under the charter's scope. Introduce the rules and context needed to follow them rather than assuming nostalgia, a particular culture or ownership of original hardware. People, creative contributions and historical circumstances belong where they explain the subject; represent underrecognised contributions within the main account rather than as a token aside.

Recall concepts without assuming prior completion. Name the useful explanation and why it connects. Do not substitute links for the immediate reasoning. Specific modern comparisons are welcome when accurate; do not force the same sidebar into each unit.

Pseudocode is a notation for reasoning, not a secretly runnable language. Say “trace this” or “the expected result is” where no execution environment exists. Avoid universal claims based on a single language or processor.

## History and evidence

Follow the evidence requirements in [the project charter](../PROJECT.md#evidence-and-responsible-history). Distinguish documented facts, recollections, disputes, allegations and interpretation. Check names, dates and credits. Do not invent motives, dialogue or anecdotes, or assert unsupported superlatives.

Do not conceal relevant wrongdoing because someone contributed technically. Explain it proportionately and in age-appropriate language, without sensationalising, excusing or adding gratuitous detail. Where the Vault supplies fuller treatment, keep enough context locally to avoid a misleading account.

Name a work accurately for the release or context being discussed and identify alternative titles when needed. British spelling is not a reason to substitute British history for another region's experience.

## Finished public writing

Published pages present the subject, not the editing process. Remove correction announcements, prompt residue, TODOs, historical policy debates and claims about unseen research. Cite original publications precisely or link to appropriate public pages. Git and pull requests hold editorial history.

Style checks can identify likely problems; judgement remains necessary. Do not remove an accurate qualification merely because a linter dislikes its wording.
