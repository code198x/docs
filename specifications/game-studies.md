# Whole-game studies

Game studies investigate what playing a game is like, how it works and how it came to exist. Follow the experience closely enough to understand what made it distinctive, including ideas that did not succeed as intended. A study is more than an illustration of one favoured technique.

Apply [the charter](../PROJECT.md), [writing voice](writing-voice.md) and the [Vault evidence standard](vault.md). Studies connect play, design, technology, art, sound, people, culture, production, distribution, marketing, business and reception where the evidence supports useful questions. These are lines of enquiry, not compulsory equal-length sections. Explain the actual release and configuration being examined; distinguish later ports, variants and recollections.

## Site placement

Game Studies initially lives within the Vault at `/vault/studies/`, with individual studies such as `/vault/studies/spacewar/` and supporting companions beneath them. This is the agreed initial placement, not a permanent restriction: the collection may become an independent section later.

Keep studies distinct from the Vault's encyclopaedic game entries. A study examines the whole experience in depth; a game entry supplies focused reference coverage. Link them where useful without duplicating their bodies or adding a new subject category. Make the collection discoverable from the Vault and relevant articles; no new primary navigation item is required now.

Keep stable study identities and canonical content independent of the current URL parent. Shared placement metadata owns generated links and breadcrumbs. If the collection moves later, retain redirects from published study and companion URLs, update discovery links and preserve one canonical destination per page. An initial Vault home does not require implementing the future move now.

Placement does not constitute historical review or deployment approval. Apply the existing human sign-off, unreviewed notice, noindex and sitemap conventions. Do not list proposed studies as coming soon.

## Follow the game outwards

Investigate its inspirations and cultural circumstances as part of every study. Look beyond earlier video games: books, film, music, art, physical play, science, work, family, place and institutions can all matter. Political and economic events may affect people's opportunities, resources, decisions or audiences. Follow demonstrated connections rather than imposing a theme on every game.

Keep three kinds of statement distinct:

- **Named influence:** a creator or contemporary document identifies a source. Attribute the statement and its date; a later memory remains a memory.
- **Documented circumstance:** evidence establishes an event, working arrangement or constraint. Explain its demonstrated effect without inventing motives or claiming that context caused a specific design choice.
- **Our comparison:** reading or playing two works suggests a useful relationship. Show the relevant detail and identify the comparison as ours. Resemblance alone does not establish influence.

Read or inspect the relevant work where practical. Check its actual title, edition and collaborative credits; the version available to us may differ from the one a creator encountered. Do not turn fictional people or a joking group name into real biographies or institutions. Treat harmful conventions in historical works proportionately under the existing voice and evidence guidance.

Start from something readers can see, hear, do or recognise: a manoeuvre, a picture, a rule, a production decision or a way the game reached them. Explain why it matters locally before offering a link. Use screenshots, diagrams and other permitted imagery to make behaviour understandable. Enthusiasm comes from the particular game and its people, not from announcing how many connections we can draw. Keep research methods and unresolved checks in the evidence record unless readers need a qualification to understand the claim.

## Lives, constraints and place

Follow people through time as far as the study needs. Childhood interests, education, work, friendship, family responsibilities, migration, conflict, loss and opportunity can affect what they can do and what they choose. Use their testimony and other evidence to establish particular relationships. Do not infer trauma, a diagnosis, a political belief or a creative motive from military service, nationality or an event alone. A life event is not a complete explanation of someone's work.

Let people change, contradict themselves and occupy different roles. Date an opinion or decision rather than give someone a permanent personality based on one interview. Preserve uncertainty, collaboration, documented harm and worthwhile work together, in proportion to the evidence. Do not manufacture either a heroic arc, a villain or a balancing flaw. Our choice of subjects and available sources also shapes the account: seek overlooked perspectives and record important gaps in the research notes.

Make constraints specific. Establish what someone wanted to achieve, which limit they encountered, what resources and choices they had, what they tried and what it cost. Distinguish technical limits, chosen rules, budgets and deadlines from restrictions imposed by exclusion, war or poverty. A workaround may demonstrate invention while also losing something. Recognise the help of collaborators, institutions and available resources; never assume hardship was necessary, beneficial or compensated for by the result. This applies the existing constraint-as-teacher principle to a concrete investigation without changing system priorities.

Investigate place at the scale the evidence supports: a family, club, workplace, city, market or state at a particular time. Japan, Britain, other European countries, the USA, South American countries and countries behind the Iron Curtain offer different cases to investigate, not fixed cultural personalities. Seek local sources, languages and perspectives; identify translation limits. Compare access and affordability, education, industry, employment, censorship, trade and currency rules, distribution, audiences and cultural influences where they explain the game. Country labels and hardware specifications alone cannot establish how everyone thought or played.

Trace routes between places as well as local differences: people moving, films and books circulating, imported machines, translation, licensing, copied software, program exchange and later ports or emulation. Explain what travelled, who could encounter it, what changed and what stayed distinctive. Legal availability, practical access and informal circulation are separate questions. Put changes in borders, trade, networks and markets on their actual local timelines. Greater exchange may spread shared conventions or enable new differences; convergence and homogeneity are questions to test, not the assumed direction of history. Do not imply that all borders opened or differences disappeared together.

Select a few useful cases rather than attach a worldwide survey to every game. Support an influence, an adaptation and a comparison separately. Keep locally important experiences visible alongside internationally successful work; access to English-language archives or commercial success is not a measure of historical importance. Carry useful gaps into the existing Vault workflow rather than adding a new regional taxonomy or promising a study for every country.

## People, institutions and the Vault

Consider Vault coverage for every named individual and institution in the study, including relevant collaborators, technicians, artists, writers, employers, research groups, user societies, publishers and preservation contributors. Record a brief disposition in the study's research notes: existing coverage to reuse, an existing candidate or issue, a useful new candidate, a section within another subject, or a reason no separate coverage is currently warranted. Distinguish a participant from an interviewer, cited authority or modern tool supplier. Citation alone does not establish involvement in the game's creation.

Before adding work, inspect current Vault titles, aliases, redirects and relevant sections, the [single candidate backlog](../vault-candidates.md), and open and closed writing issues. Check retained candidate work on other active branches before allocating stable IDs. A mention within an entry may supply enough context; it does not automatically amount to a biography. Do not duplicate subjects under an abbreviation, nickname or a spelling variant.

Add useful missing subjects to vault-candidates.csv using its existing fields and triage rules. Record the reader question, source leads, the referring study and possible backlinks from existing Vault entries. Keep proposed destinations out of public links until coverage exists. The study's disposition audit points to those records; it is not a second candidate backlog. Commission an article only after triage establishes its scope, evidence and value. There is no biography quota, automatic issue batch or new institution category.

Link reviewed work back in both directions where it helps readers. A study can send someone to an institution's history, while the institution's entry can offer the game as one concrete example. Historical articles retain their own subject focus; they do not become descriptions of the Code198x curriculum. Connections can lead to Maths for Games, Game Feel and other Craft explanations without claiming a planned lesson already exists.

## Onward explanations and practical work

Keep explanations of the studied game's controls, rules and behaviour local enough to follow play. Use brief onward links for broader topics such as control devices; a game study need not become a survey of peripherals. Put detailed accounts of later versions and descendants in their own Vault entries, with concise references from the study. Identify adaptations, creator-attributed influences and our comparisons separately, including any intermediate game in the chain. Link actual coverage rather than proposed routes.

Studies do not each require a curriculum game or remake. Select practical examples when they serve a clear teaching purpose: a deliberately limited behaviour experiment can be sufficient. A fuller original game can grow in playable stages when it earns a place in progression. Multiple implementations can be worth considering when different machines or languages reveal useful choices; they are not an automatic porting programme or a change to current delivery priorities.

Use our own implementation and original presentation. Check source licences before reusing code, and permissions before reusing protected artwork, sound, text or other expression. Assess the proposed name, branding and presentation separately before publication; acknowledgement does not substitute for permission. A historical game's availability, age or an imitation elsewhere is not a rights clearance. The existing capture/imagery decisions still govern historical illustrations. Record unresolved rights questions before choosing what to reuse, without claiming that a code rewrite alone resolves every issue.

## Bound the work

Begin with the agreed vintage studies: Spacewar!, Monty Mole, Elite and Lemmings. Investigate each as a whole game rather than assigning it one exclusive concept. These are an initial selection, not a limit of twelve games or a restriction against later-era comparisons. Rachel remains outside this first selection.

Apply the charter's admission test to extensions. Independent cultural and historical value can warrant coverage; an immediate programming exercise is not required. Current delivery priorities and the principles remain intact. Wider coverage does not promise another system track, emulator capability or a new navigation collection.

Spacewar is the documentation pilot. Its agreed initial home is the Vault; its interactive examples remain explicitly deferred. Static explanations and companions can be developed and reviewed now. Agree any later interactive work and publication separately; do not let the study quietly commission a new course or feature.

Before publication, verify claims, source interpretation, calculations, links, imagery rights, credits and the actual rendered page. Record what was executed and what remains to be tried with people. Automated checks and an author's review do not replace the existing human publication sign-off.
