# Touchdown — lesson brief

**Status:** Agreed eleven-lesson sequence. Sixteen teaching checkpoints and a separate input diagnostic are implemented and verified; the overview and eleven lesson drafts are ready for review. See the [lesson implementation record](lessons.md).
**Route:** Meet BASIC → Bright Spark → Volley → Touchdown.
**Target:** Stock 48K ZX Spectrum, PAL, Sinclair BASIC, keyboard and tape save/load.
**Scope and evidence:** [Game brief](brief.md), [prototype and play review](prototype.md), and maintained samples under `code-samples/sinclair-zx-spectrum/basic/touchdown/prototype/`.

## What the learner will finish

A small lander crosses a fixed screen of uneven terrain and touches down on a marked pad. Gravity changes its descent speed; the player steers directly with O/P and burns limited fuel with Space. Steering and thrust work together. Contact gives a reason for success or failure, R offers another attempt, and Q leaves cleanly after release. The learner creates the craft and flame, draws the landscape from its height data, and saves a game that can be loaded and played again.

The native prototype was reported to work well and to be quite hard. Build towards that challenge through simpler playable situations. Begin with vertical landing and unlimited thrust. Add retry before limited fuel, then a generous pad on flat ground. Introduce uneven terrain only after the player can steer and brake together. Keep the current full-game settings as the comparison target; no difficulty reduction, extra mode or difficulty menu is settled by this brief.

Eleven lessons are used because they separate the important changes, not because the module needs a particular length. If simultaneous input cannot be explained and exercised comfortably in one lesson, split it rather than hide a routine or compress its explanation.

## Starting knowledge and representation

Start a new program; no unexplained source from Volley is required. Recall assignments, conditions, FOR/NEXT, PRINT AT, polling, signed movement and consecutive erase/draw locally. Volley supplies useful experience of those ideas, but attendance is not a prerequisite. Neither assembly nor Foundations is required.

Use the prototype's hundredths-of-a-row representation from the first lesson. For example, a stored position of 450 displays in row `INT (450/100)`, which is row 4; adding a step of 20 gives 470, still in row 4. Keep the changing number visible beside the character so an unchanged picture does not suggest the program has stopped. Explain multiplication, division and INT here, before introducing acceleration. This is a chosen scale for the model, not a claim that Sinclair BASIC is using an integer-only number type or that the units are metres.

Screen rows increase downwards. The displayed row is derived from the model and never replaces it. With ground beginning in row 20, the one-cell craft makes contact at position 1900: its feet then meet the first solid row. Retain this convention through the array and terrain lessons.

Use black sky, bright PAPER-coloured ground and a contrasting pad from their first appearance. Begin with an ordinary `A` character for the craft; use a visible alternate character during thrust. Custom graphics later replace that deliberate provisional symbol. The game remains understandable without colour or sound.

## Lesson sequence

Each lesson continues from the preceding complete checkpoint. Titles identify the review drafts; exact line numbers and add/replace/delete sets are recorded against the maintained teaching sources.

| Lesson | Playable or inspectable result | New understanding and concrete changes | Check of understanding and execution |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Let the craft fall** | A visible craft descends at a constant rate onto flat coloured ground, then stops. RUN repeats the demonstration. | Explain the position scale and derived screen row; keep a constant step. Draw ground once, calculate the next position, clamp contact, then erase/draw consecutively. Recall PRINT AT's row/column order and the one-cell footprint. | Trace 450 → 470 → 490 → 510 and predict when the picture changes. Check contact at 1900, clearing of old positions and valid screen coordinates. |
| 2. **Let gravity change the speed** | The unattended fall accelerates; the speed instrument shows why successive steps grow. | Replace the constant step with velocity and a small gravity increment. Explain the order: change speed, then change position. Add a documented downward speed cap. Keep the same starting point, flat ground and contact rule. | Trace the first four updates from speed zero. Explain why adding gravity directly to position would produce a different motion. Check that a step crossing the contact height cannot pass through the ground. |
| 3. **Brake before you arrive** | Space brakes the descent; a vertical attempt ends in a safe landing or a hard impact. Thrust is unlimited. | Poll Space with INKEY$. Thrust changes velocity, not position directly. Introduce upward velocity, the ceiling clamp and the safe downward-speed interval. Show thrust with a provisional alternate character in the same cell. | Explain why firing the engine can leave the craft moving downwards. Compare an early burn with a late one. Demonstrate a safe landing and a hard landing; check held/released thrust and ascent at the ceiling. |
| 4. **Make another attempt** | Instructions, S to launch, a result, R to retry and Q to quit make the vertical lander a complete small game. | Recall GO SUB/RETURN where useful and separate initialisation, flight and result handling. Explain AND locally in the menu-choice filter. Name every changing value that retry resets. Wait for release at launch, retry and exit; do not leave an unfinished call on restart. | Hold S, R and Q and explain why one press must not leak into another phase or the BASIC prompt. Complete safe and failed attempts, retry both, and confirm position, speed, thrust display and instructions reset. |
| 5. **Make each burn count** | The same vertical game has visible limited fuel. An empty tank stops thrust but the attempt continues. | Add one resource counter and a single thrust decision used by movement, fuel consumption and the flame. Recall AND from the menu filter and explain its use in the fuel condition. Add fuel to retry's reset set. | Trace a held burn with one unit left: it is spent once, then later updates fall without thrust. Demonstrate both an empty-tank crash and a controlled empty-tank coast to a safe landing. Verify no negative fuel or stale flame. |
| 6. **Reach a landing pad** | O/P move the craft towards a generous marked pad on flat ground. Braking and steering are initially separate actions. Off-pad contact has its own result. | Add horizontal position, one-column steps, screen clamps and an inclusive pad interval. Recall interval checks from Volley without assuming them. Use one INKEY$ reading and explicit one-key-at-a-time controls at this checkpoint. Start with a short lateral approach. | Predict both pad-edge contacts and adjacent misses. Show that landing slowly beside the pad still fails. Try a steering key with Space and observe the limitation; label this as a temporary control restriction, not player error. |
| 7. **Steer and thrust together** | The flat-pad game accepts O/P and Space simultaneously. Opposing steering keys cancel. | Replace only flight input with two IN reads. Explain a port address, the selected keyboard half-rows, active-low bits and how INT/remainders extract the two steering states and the thrust state. Keep the movement, fuel and landing rules unchanged. | Work through the four O/P bit pairs and combine each with Space. Check held, tapped, released, simultaneous and opposing controls. Explain why comparing the entire returned byte with one constant is unreliable. |
| 8. **Give every column a ground height** | The same flat landing site is drawn and checked from a numeric array. Changing one height changes both its visible surface and contact. | Introduce DIM, a numeric array element, and the mapping from screen column `c` to `h(c+1)`. Initially fill all 32 entries with the existing flat height using FOR/NEXT. Replace the ground constant in drawing and downward contact together. | Explain why an array index differs from its screen column. Compare the complete flat version before/after the representation change. Raise one off-pad column and verify drawing/contact agree; state that entering its side is the next lesson's unfinished rule. |
| 9. **Read the shape of the site** | A fixed uneven landscape affects the approach. Flying into a hillside crashes, including while climbing. | Load the height array with READ/DATA/RESTORE. Explain data order and when it is read, then use the same values for terrain drawing and collision. Add horizontal destination-height checks before downward contact; retain the old clear cell on a side collision. Keep the pad level. | Trace an ascending side collision and a fast downward crossing. Explain why checking only the new row is insufficient when moving sideways. Change one terrain height and predict its two consequences. Check the original site survives repeated attempts without exhausting DATA. |
| 10. **Draw the craft and its flame** | An original custom-character lander and flame replace the provisional symbols without changing the flight rules or footprint. | Reuse bit values from keyboard work and DATA reading from the site. Construct eight pixel rows, convert them to byte values, and explain POKE, USR with a string argument and CHR$ locally. Build the resting craft first, then its burning variant. Keep both inside one cell. | Change one row of the design and predict the pixels. Explain why a flame below the collision footprint could overwrite the ground. Compare both states against the sky and pad, and verify no landscape damage when they change. |
| 11. **Finish, test and keep Touchdown** | The full landing challenge has clear instructions and outcomes, short result sounds, reliable retry/exit, and a saved tape that reopens as a playable game. | Review the complete loop and reset contract. Add and explain outcome BEEP commands only after flight stops; retain silent flight. Compare pad width and approach settings one at a time, then select the final reviewed site. Save, reload, replay, and supply original-asset/licence credits. | Explain the first change to make when an approach is too hard, and what observation would support it. Run the end-to-end checks below. Reopen the exported tape in a fresh session and play, retry and quit. |

Lesson 4 deliberately fulfils a smaller vertical game's complete-play promise. Lesson 11 fulfils the agreed moving-lander-and-landscape promise. Earlier programs are useful games and experiments, not disposable code to replace with a hidden final engine.

## Difficulty progression to test

Keep gravity, thrust strength, velocity limits and position scale steady while adding the new mechanics. Otherwise the learner cannot tell whether a result changed because of their code, the new rule or a different model.

The first lateral game uses an eight-cell pad (columns 20–27) on flat ground and starts at column 18. A successful single-key approach is verified; the practice settings still need learner review. Do not assert that the wider pad is sufficient to make the game easy. Lesson 6 must allow a successful approach with its stated one-key-at-a-time controls; if it does not, adjust the initial approach or split the control demonstration before proceeding.

Develop lesson 9 in separate runnable steps: first the DATA-backed flat site, then one raised obstacle with its side-contact rule, then the complete skyline. Test a longer lateral approach on flat ground before combining it with the ridge. Keep a generous pad during this investigation. Lesson 11 can compare the prototype's four-cell pad and starting position with the practice version, changing one setting at a time. A successful demonstration by an automated controller is not the criterion for choosing the learner's final difficulty.

The current prototype supplies a tested comparison: starting column 6, position 400, 45 fuel units, gravity +2, thrust −6, velocity limits −30 to +60, safe downward speed 0–12 and pad columns 23–26. These settings remain provisional. The lesson brief does not authorise a difficulty menu, procedural terrain, horizontal momentum or new levels.

## Keeping simultaneous input understandable

Lesson 7 is the main teaching risk. Give it a short inspectable keyboard display before replacing the game's input lines. Label left, right and thrust separately and let the learner hold each combination while looking at the corresponding values. This diagnostic is a maintained checkpoint with no moving craft to distract from the observation.

Explain that the Spectrum reports pressed keys as zero bits. For the O/P half-row, the low two bits are `11` for neither, `10` for P only, `01` for O only, and `00` for both, written high bit first. Space is read from a different half-row. Other returned bits are not part of those controls, so the game must extract the relevant bits rather than compare whole bytes.

Work through the arithmetic used by the actual source: `keys-2*INT(keys/2)` leaves the low bit; applying the same grouping idea after division by two exposes the next bit. Explain each intermediate value before condensing the expression. Sinclair BASIC's AND here is a logical condition, not a substitute for bit masking.

Name why ports 57342 and 32766 select the needed half-rows, with a small keyboard/address diagram and the original manual's mapping. Explain the address as an argument to IN, separately from the byte returned. Binary weights and the selected row are essential local explanations; an assembly-language detour is not. Verify worked bit examples against the source and emulator. If this explanation crowds out the experiment, split the diagnostic and game integration into two lessons.
