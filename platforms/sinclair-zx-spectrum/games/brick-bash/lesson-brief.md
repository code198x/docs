## What develops here

Start a new program. Show its first short listing in full, without instructions to add every line. Recall variables, signed movement, candidate positions, subroutines, arrays and custom characters locally. Readers do not import an earlier game's engine or need to remember its line numbers.

The central relationship is between **pictures, positions and stored rules**. The paddle and bricks are printed as characters; the ball moves in pixels. A picture tells the player where a brick is, its rectangle tells the program whether the ball touches it, and an array remembers whether it still exists. Those three representations must agree, but none substitutes for the others.

The second relationship is between catching and aiming. Begin with a predictable bounce, then let contact position choose a return. Keep this distinction visible in play and in questions: reaching the ball prevents a miss; positioning within the paddle shapes the next journey. The user's difficulty observation does not authorise wider paddles, extra lives or slower movement.

## Explain the coordinate conversions

Use the game's actual values and a labelled diagram before presenting arithmetic. These are program-design choices checked against the source, not new hardware claims.

- A paddle at p=14 begins at pixel 112 and ends at 143. Its attached ball begins at x=127 and occupies 127–128.
- Brick row r is printed at character row 2+2*r; its pixel bottom is 152-16*r. Brick column c starts at pixel 32*c and spans through 32*c+23. Row 1, column 1 therefore occupies x=32–55, y=136–143.
- For the ball's candidate lower-left position tx/ty, use br=1+INT((143-ty)/16) and bc=1+INT((tx+1-32)/32). The +1 admits the ball's rightmost pixel. These select a possible brick, not proof of contact.
- Reject br outside 1–3 or bc outside 1–6 before indexing. Reject a vertical gap when ty+1 < 152-16*br and a horizontal gap when tx > 32*bc+23. Only then ask whether g(br,bc) is live.

Work through boundary examples: (31,136) touches the first brick with one pixel; (30,136) does not; (56,136) lies in the horizontal gap; (32,135) touches its bottom with the ball's upper pixel; (32,134) misses it. The spacing and two-pixel ball mean no candidate overlaps two neighbouring brick rectangles. This lookup is specific to this wall layout, not a general rectangle search.

For aiming, name L=8*p in the explanation. A catch returns left if nx+1 < L+11, right if nx > L+20, otherwise vertically. Thus lower-left ball coordinates L+9/L+10 straddle the left/centre decision, and L+20/L+21 straddle the centre/right decision. Explain the use of different ball edges instead of drawing misleading equal thirds.

No new website component is required. Use maintained source excerpts, a pixel/character diagram, a rectangle-overlap trace and the existing question component. Useful questions include why a two-pixel ball needs x+1; why a candidate brick can still be a gap; why disappearance must change stored state; and why a successful catch can still be a poor shot.
